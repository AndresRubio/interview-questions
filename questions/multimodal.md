# Multimodal

Everything else in this bank assumes text in and text out. This file covers what changes when inputs are images, documents, audio or video: how a vision-language model is wired, why an image is priced in tokens and how to do that arithmetic, OCR pipelines versus reading the page as an image (with multimodal retrieval), measuring visual hallucination, voice-agent latency budgets, video sampling, and the tasks where VLMs still fail. For general eval methodology and judges see [evaluation.md](evaluation.md); for chunking and text RAG see [retrieval-and-rag.md question 5](retrieval-and-rag.md#5-how-would-you-chunk-a-corpus-that-has-its-own-published-structure); for the cost-arithmetic habit see [inference-and-systems.md question 2](inference-and-systems.md#2-do-the-cost-arithmetic-out-loud-50000-daily-users-an-agent-averaging-12-llm-calls-per-session-4k-input-and-500-output-tokens-per-call) and [cost-and-caching.md](cost-and-caching.md).

### 1. How does a vision-language model actually "see"? Walk through the architecture.

`single-source`

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The common open design has three parts: a **vision encoder**, a **projector**, and an **LLM**.

1. **Vision encoder.** A ViT, usually pretrained CLIP-style: contrastive training that predicts which caption goes with which image, on 400 million image-text pairs in the original CLIP paper (arXiv 2103.00020, ICML 2021). The image is cut into fixed-size patches, and each patch becomes an embedding that already sits in a space aligned with language.
2. **Projector.** This maps the patch embeddings into the LLM's token-embedding space. LLaVA (arXiv 2304.08485, NeurIPS 2023) used a single linear layer. LLaVA-1.5 (arXiv 2310.03744, CVPR 2024) moved to an MLP on CLIP-ViT-L-336px and reported that this "surprisingly powerful" fully-connected connector reached state of the art on 11 benchmarks, with the 13B model trained in about a day on one 8-A100 node.
3. **LLM.** It receives the projected patches as ordinary tokens mixed in with the text tokens and attends over all of them.

The usual training recipe is to align the projector first (encoder and LLM frozen) and then instruction-tune end to end. LLaVA's instruction data was generated by a text-only GPT-4.

**The senior instinct.** Three production consequences follow from this design:
- **Images are tokens, so they cost tokens.** They take context and prefill time, and the price scales with resolution (question 2).
- **Everything goes through a resolution bottleneck.** The encoder sees a downscaled image, so fine print and small objects can be lost before the LLM ever sees them.
- **The LLM does the reasoning, and it has a language prior.** When the visual signal is weak, the model fills the gap with what is *likely*, which is how you get visual hallucination (question 4) and failures on counting and geometry (question 7).

</details>

### 2. A user uploads a 1920x1080 screenshot. What does it cost in tokens, and how does that differ across providers?

`single-source`

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Providers use different formulas, so the same image has a different token count on each, and you should check the current docs rather than rely on memory. As of the official docs fetched 2026-09-25:

- **Anthropic** counts 28x28-pixel patches: `⌈width/28⌉ × ⌈height/28⌉` visual tokens. The cap depends on the tier: the standard tier allows a 1568 px long edge and 1568 visual tokens; the high-resolution tier (Claude 4.7 and later) allows 2576 px and 4784 tokens. The docs' own table gives **1560 tokens** for 1920x1080 on the standard tier (downscaled to 1456x819) and **2691 tokens** on the high-resolution tier (not resized). They also note that high-resolution images can use "roughly three times more" tokens.
- **OpenAI** uses two schemes. For the tile-based models, `detail: "low"` costs only the base tokens (85 for gpt-4o and gpt-4.1; gpt-4o-mini is higher, at 2833 base tokens, so 85 isn't universal). `high` fits the image inside 2048x2048, scales the short side down to 768, then charges per 512-px tile (170 for gpt-4o, 5667 per tile for gpt-4o-mini) plus the base. Newer models use 32x32 patches with a patch budget and a per-model multiplier. For example, a 1024x1024 image is 1024 patches, billed `ceil(1024 × 1.2) = 1229` tokens on a 1.2× model. The API rejects any image over 30,000 patches.
- **Gemini** charges 258 tokens for an image of 384 px or less on both sides, tiled into 768x768 tiles at 258 tokens each — this is the scheme the tokens page gives for the models it covers. A `media_resolution` parameter caps the tokens spent per image or frame; on Gemini 3, the media-resolution page gives defaults of 1120 tokens/image, down to 280 at `low`, up to 2240 at `ultra_high`.

**The senior instinct.** Treat resolution as a cost setting you choose deliberately:
- **Downscale on the client** to the smallest size that keeps the detail you need. Anthropic's docs recommend resizing before upload to cut both cost and latency.
- **Match detail to the task.** Low detail is fine for classification or "what's in this photo". Keep high detail for dense documents, small text and computer-use screenshots.
- **Watch for accumulation in agents.** A computer-use loop that keeps resending screenshots grows its context by thousands of tokens per step. Anthropic also applies a stricter per-image size limit once a request holds more than 20 images, counting images from earlier turns and inside tool results. Old screenshots are the first thing to trim (see [context-engineering.md](context-engineering.md)).

</details>

### 3. You need to ingest 200,000 PDFs (contracts, scanned invoices, slide decks) for RAG. OCR pipeline, or let a VLM read the pages?

`single-source`

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Use both, with a router in front. The real design question is which pages deserve the expensive path.

**Local parsing and OCR first.** Most born-digital PDFs already contain a text layer. LlamaIndex's LiteParse (open source, [github.com/run-llama/liteparse](https://github.com/run-llama/liteparse), fetched 2026-09-25) is a representative example. It extracts spatial text with bounding boxes through PDFium, which its README puts at about 2-5 ms per page, with no LLM and no cloud dependency. It runs OCR (bundled Tesseract, or a pluggable OCR server) only where it is needed, and its "complexity detection" feature checks cheaply whether a document needs heavier parsing, so you can route, reject, or estimate cost before a full parse. The README is candid about the limits: dense tables, multi-column layouts, charts, handwriting and scanned PDFs get "significantly better results" with a heavier (in their case hosted) parser.

**VLM-native reading for the hard tail.** Send page images to a VLM when layout carries meaning: tables whose cell relationships matter, charts, forms, handwriting. This costs roughly a thousand or more tokens per page (question 2) plus generation, and it adds a new failure mode: the output is fluent, plausible and wrong, where OCR would have produced visible garbage.

**Retrieving on the image itself.** ColPali (arXiv 2407.01449, ICLR 2025) skips text extraction for retrieval. A VLM embeds each page *image* into multiple vectors, and queries are matched by late interaction (ColBERT-style). On the authors' ViDoRe benchmark it "largely outperforms" text-extraction pipelines while being simpler and end-to-end trainable. The cost is storage: you keep many vectors per page instead of one per chunk, and you still need a VLM to generate the answer from the retrieved page images.

**The senior instinct.** The design has three parts:
1. **Triage every page cheaply.** Use the text layer where one exists, run OCR only on scanned pages, and escalate a page to a VLM only when complexity detection flags it.
2. **Keep bounding boxes** so every answer can cite a location on the page.
3. **Evaluate per document class** (invoices separately from slide decks) against a small hand-labelled set, because one aggregate accuracy number hides the class where the pipeline fails.

</details>

### 4. How do you measure whether a VLM is hallucinating what's in the image?

`single-source`

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Don't grade free-form captions with a text metric. POPE (arXiv 2305.10355, EMNLP 2023) showed that the older caption-based measures shift with the instruction and the model's generation style. Its fix is **polling**: ask a batch of yes/no questions of the form "Is there a {object} in the image?", with ground-truth objects as positives and absent objects as negatives. The negatives come from three sampling strategies, and the comparison between them is the point:

- **random**: any object not in the image;
- **popular**: objects that are frequent across the dataset;
- **adversarial**: objects that often *co-occur* with the objects actually in the image.

The paper found that models most often hallucinate objects that are frequent in instruction data or that co-occur with what is present. That is the language prior at work (question 1). A large gap between the random and adversarial scores tells you the model is guessing from context rather than looking. Also report the **yes-ratio**, because a model that answers "yes" too readily can post decent accuracy while hallucinating heavily.

**The senior instinct.** A public benchmark checks the model. Your product needs its own version of the same idea:
- Build probes from your own domain: "Does this invoice have a line item for X?", where X is plausible but absent.
- Include unanswerable images, such as blank or cropped pages or the wrong document, and score whether the model abstains.
- For extraction, compare fields against ground truth rather than having a judge read the prose. Judge-based evaluation has its own biases (see [evaluation.md](evaluation.md)), and a judge that sees the image can share the same visual blind spots.

</details>

### 5. Design a voice agent for phone support. Cascaded STT → LLM → TTS, or a speech-to-speech model?

`single-source`

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Frame it as a **latency budget**, not a model choice. Hamza Farooq's deep dive on voice agents (Jul 27, 2026, [boringbot.substack.com](https://boringbot.substack.com/p/voice-agents-a-deep-dive), fetched 2026-09-25; practitioner essay) puts the budget at roughly **700-800 ms per conversational turn** before a conversation stops feeling human. It treats cascaded versus speech-to-speech, open versus closed, and build versus buy as decisions about where to spend those milliseconds. Treat the number as a practitioner rule of thumb, not a measured standard.

**Cascaded (voice-activity detection → STT → LLM → TTS).**
- *Pros:* each stage can be inspected. You have a transcript to log, a text point where guardrails and compliance checks run before anything is spoken, and a separate choice of best component for each stage.
- *Cons:* every handoff adds latency. You also lose paralinguistic signal (tone, hesitation) at the transcription step.

**Speech-to-speech (native audio models behind realtime APIs).**
- *Pros:* removing the handoffs brings latency down, and the model can respond to prosody.
- *Cons:* the checkpoints disappear along with the handoffs. It is harder to block a non-compliant utterance before it is spoken, harder to debug, and you depend on one vendor's model for every stage.

**The senior instinct.** Choose by the domain's tolerance for error:
- **Regulated support** (finance, health) usually wants a cascade with streaming at every stage. Begin TTS on the first sentence of the reply, and use endpointing that doesn't wait for long silences. You pay some latency to keep the audit point.
- **Low-stakes, latency-critical** conversation can take speech-to-speech.

Either way, measure time-to-first-audio at the p95, not the mean. Handle barge-in (the user interrupting) as a first-class state. And note that a tool call in the middle of a turn can use up the entire budget, so say something ("let me check that") while the tool runs.

</details>

### 6. Someone wants to ask questions about hour-long recorded meetings. How do you think about video cost?

`single-source`

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Do the arithmetic before you design anything. Gemini's docs (fetched 2026-09-25) state that video is sampled at **1 frame per second** by default. The rate itself differs by page: the tokens page gives **263 tokens/second** under static processing, while the video-understanding page gives **~100 tokens/second at the default (low) media resolution** and **~300 tokens/second at high**. At the tokens-page rate, one hour is therefore about 947,000 tokens, close to a million, before anyone asks a question. On the media-resolution page, 70 tokens/frame is already the default (covering unspecified, `low`, and `medium`), and setting `media_resolution` to `high` raises it to 280 tokens/frame — the opposite of "lowering resolution cuts tokens." The docs also describe an agentic processing mode that inspects the timeline selectively, with variable token usage.

Watch for inconsistencies even inside official docs. The same Gemini documentation gives audio as 32 tokens/second on the token-counting page and 25 tokens/second on the media-resolution page. Quote the page you actually billed against, and measure with the token-counting endpoint.

**The trade-off.** A fixed frame rate is a bet about what matters:
- At 1 fps, anything that happens between samples is invisible: a slide shown for half a second, a brief gesture.
- For meetings, the information is mostly in the **audio**. It is much cheaper to transcribe, retrieve over the transcript, and pull frames only where the transcript points to something visual ("as you can see on this chart").

**The senior instinct.**
1. **Pick the modality that carries the information.** Transcript first; keyframes on scene change rather than on a fixed clock.
2. **Index once, query many times.** Don't resend the whole video for every question. Store transcript chunks and keyframe embeddings, and retrieve the relevant segment.
3. **Use native long-video context** only when temporal reasoning across the whole clip is the task (for example "when did the speaker first contradict the earlier number?"), and cache it if the same video will get many queries (see [cost-and-caching.md](cost-and-caching.md)).

</details>

### 7. Where do multimodal models still fail, and how do you design around it?

`single-source`

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

They are weakest at **precise low-level perception**: counting, spatial relationships, overlapping geometry, reading exact values off charts. Strong scores on general VQA benchmarks can hide this. "Vision language models are blind" (arXiv 2407.06581, preprint) built BlindTest, seven tasks that are trivial for humans: do two circles overlap, how many times do two lines intersect, which letter is circled, how many circles are in an Olympic-like logo. Four state-of-the-art VLMs averaged **58.07%**. The best, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, reached 77.84%, against an expected human accuracy of 100%. Accuracy approached 100% once the shapes were spread further apart. Linear probes showed the *vision encoder* held enough information to solve the tasks, so the failure lies in the language model's decoding of that information, not in the model's eyesight. Those numbers are for 2024 models. Re-run the idea on the model you actually ship rather than assuming it has been fixed or that it still holds.

**Designing around it:**
- **Don't ask the VLM for exact numbers from a chart when the underlying data exists.** Get the table or CSV, or have the model write code that reads the data.
- **Counting and localisation:** use a detector or segmentation model for the count, and let the VLM explain the result.
- **Dense or small content:** crop and zoom (tile the region of interest at higher resolution) rather than sending the whole page downscaled (question 2).
- **Screenshots and computer use:** check coordinates against the accessibility tree or the DOM where one exists.
- **Evaluate the failure class explicitly.** Put counting, spatial and chart-reading items into your eval set (question 4), because aggregate accuracy will not show them.

**The senior instinct** is to treat the VLM as a strong generalist reader with poor precision, and to route precise perception to deterministic tools, the same way you would not ask an LLM to do long arithmetic in its head.

</details>
